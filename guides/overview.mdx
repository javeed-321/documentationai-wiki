## Overview

Stripe gives you a single platform for accepting money, moving money, and automating how you earn revenue. You can start with one capability, then add more as your business grows. That makes it a practical fit for online stores, software businesses, marketplaces, and services that need to handle payments at scale.

Use this page as a starter guide for evaluating the core building blocks: payments, billing, issuing, Connect, and Treasury. Each section below explains what it does, where it fits, and what to consider before you implement it.

## Online, in-app, and in-person payments

Stripe Payments helps you accept payments where your customers already are. You can take checkout flows online, embed payment experiences in an app, or process card-present payments in person. The platform is designed to support global expansion, with many currencies and payment methods available through a consistent API-driven workflow.

A good starting point is to map your payment surface. Online commerce often needs optimized checkout and retries. In-app purchases need mobile-friendly flows. In-person transactions need terminal support and consistent reconciliation with the rest of your business.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Online" icon="globe">
    Use online payments when customers buy through a browser.

    - Optimize checkout for conversion.
    - Offer local payment methods where they improve acceptance.

  </Tab>
  <Tab title="In-app" icon="smartphone">

    - Reduce friction for recurring or one-click purchases.

    - Reconcile store sales with online sales in one system.
    - Keep reporting and refund workflows consistent across channels.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Flexible billing and invoicing

Stripe Billing supports recurring revenue models, usage-based pricing, and invoice-driven collections. That matters when your pricing is not a simple one-time charge. You can bill monthly, charge by consumption, or combine fixed and variable components in one plan.

The main design choice is how your customers pay over time. Subscription businesses usually need automatic renewals, proration, and dunning. Usage-based businesses need metering and clear thresholds. Invoice-based businesses often need approval workflows and flexible payment timing.

## Issuing, Connect, and Treasury

Stripe also supports financial operations beyond checkout. Issuing lets you create and manage cards for employees, contractors, or customers. Connect helps you build a platform that moves money between multiple parties. Treasury gives you tools for storing funds and managing cash flows inside a financial workflow.

These capabilities are especially useful when your business is not just a merchant, but a platform or financial workflow. Marketplaces, SaaS platforms, and service providers often use them to reduce operational friction and to keep the full money movement lifecycle in one place.

### Card issuing and platform payouts

Issuing is useful when you want to control spending with virtual or physical cards. Connect is useful when you need to pay sellers, split funds, or manage platform economics. Treasury is useful when you want to organize balances and move funds with more control.

## Stablecoin and crypto money movement

If you are exploring new money movement models, Stripe can also support emerging financial infrastructure such as stablecoins and crypto-related use cases. These are usually most relevant when your application needs programmable settlement, cross-border efficiency, or new types of stored value.

Keep this section tied to a real business need. You should define why you need this capability, which jurisdictions apply, and how you will handle risk, reporting, and customer support. For many teams, the right approach is to pilot carefully before expanding.

### Best practices

- Start with one core use case before adding more products.
- Keep your payment, billing, and reporting model aligned.
- Review refund, dispute, and reconciliation workflows early.
- Confirm compliance and regional requirements before launch.
- Use clear internal ownership for finance, operations, and support.

## Evaluation examples

Use the examples below to shape your own rollout plan.

### Starter evaluation path

1. Accept payments for one primary channel.
2. Add billing if your revenue is recurring or usage-based.
3. Introduce issuing or Connect if your business manages spend or multiple parties.
4. Extend into Treasury or other financial workflows when you need tighter control over funds.

### Typical business fits

| Business type | Best starting point | Why it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E-commerce | Payments | You need fast checkout and broad payment method coverage. |
| SaaS | Billing | You need recurring revenue and invoicing. |
| Marketplace | Connect | You need to route money between buyers, sellers, and the platform. |
| On-demand platform | Payments and Connect | You need to collect money and coordinate payouts. |

## Choosing what to implement first

If you are evaluating Stripe for the first time, begin with the part of your revenue model that creates the most immediate value. For many teams, that is payments. For subscription businesses, billing may be the better first step. For platforms, money movement and multi-party payout logic often matter most.

The best implementation is the one that matches how your business earns money today, while leaving room for the next model you may need tomorrow.

<Callout kind="tip">
When you draft your rollout plan, write down the payment methods, billing rules, payout rules, and reporting needs before you start building.
</Callout>
